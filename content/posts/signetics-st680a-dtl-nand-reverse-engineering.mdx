## What happened
The ST680A is a four‑NAND‑gate chip fabricated with diode‑transistor logic, a technique that predates modern CMOS. Its plastic DIP package, introduced by Signetics in late 1965, replaced earlier metal cans and ceramic packs, making mass production cheaper and board layout simpler. CuriousMarc opened the chip using a hot‑air decapper, then cleaned residual epoxy with a few drops of boiling sulfuric acid to expose the silicon die and bond wires.

Microscopic inspection showed the classic DTL layout: input diodes feeding a common transistor that pulls the output low only when all inputs are high. The four gates share a common bias network, illustrating how designers maximized functionality with minimal components. The article documents each transistor, diode, and resistor, confirming the chip’s operation as a true NAND array.

## Why it matters
Revealing the ST680A’s internals bridges a gap between early IC engineering and today’s hobbyist practices. It illustrates the evolution from discrete diode‑transistor stages to integrated CMOS, helping enthusiasts appreciate the trade‑offs in speed, power, and layout that shaped modern chips. Moreover, the reverse‑engineering process showcases safe, reproducible techniques for opening epoxy‑encapsulated parts—knowledge that can rescue other obscure vintage components from obscurity.

## How to think about it
When approaching a vintage IC, start by documenting its package and pinout before attempting any physical opening. Use a low‑temperature hot‑air tool to soften the epoxy, then clean the die with minimal acid—just enough to remove residue without damaging the silicon. Photograph each step, then map visible structures to known DTL schematics. Finally, compare your observations against historical data to confirm functionality, and consider replicating the circuit on a breadboard for hands‑on testing.

<Callout type="warning">Handle sulfuric or nitric acid in a well‑ventilated area, wear gloves and eye protection, and dispose of waste responsibly.</Callout>

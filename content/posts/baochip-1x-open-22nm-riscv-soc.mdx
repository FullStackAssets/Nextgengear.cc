The open‑hardware community got a fresh boost on November 12 when Andrew “bunnie” Huang announced the Baochip‑1x, a 22 nm RISC‑V system‑on‑chip now available through Crowd Supply. The chip ships with an on‑chip MMU, true‑random number generator, AES engine and a suite of cryptographic hash units, all packaged in a layout that can be inspected with a low‑cost infrared camera. It is designed to run Xous, a Rust‑based microkernel OS originally built for Bunnie’s Precursor board. By exposing traditionally “real‑computer” features in a tiny, inspectable silicon die, Baochip‑1x promises new possibilities for high‑assurance embedded devices.
<Callout type="takeaway">Baochip‑1x brings desktop‑class memory management and built‑in security to an open, inspectable RISC‑V SoC.</Callout>
## What happened
On 2026‑11‑12 the Baochip‑1x was listed on Crowd Supply, and early adopters reported receiving the Dabao board that houses the chip. The SoC is fabricated in a 22 nm process and includes a memory‑management unit (MMU) that enables virtual memory, a feature rarely found in low‑power embedded silicon.
In addition to the MMU, the chip integrates a true‑random number generator, AES encryption accelerator and multiple cryptographic hash units, all exposed through a transparent hardware interface. Its layout is deliberately IR‑transparent, allowing users to verify transistor placement with inexpensive infrared cameras, a design choice highlighted in Bunnie’s IRIS inspection documentation.
## Why it matters
The inclusion of an MMU and hardware security blocks lowers the barrier for developers to build tamper‑resistant, formally verified systems without resorting to proprietary silicon. It also aligns with the growing demand for open‑source hardware in regulated sectors such as aerospace, medical devices and critical infrastructure, where auditability is a legal requirement.

## How to think about it
If you are evaluating Baochip‑1x for a new product, start by mapping your security requirements to the on‑chip primitives (TRNG, AES, hash) and decide whether you need virtual memory. Verify that your firmware stack can run Xous or port another Rust‑based microkernel; the existing README files provide a solid migration path. Finally, budget for a simple IR camera and allocate time for silicon‑level inspection if compliance audits are part of your roadmap.
